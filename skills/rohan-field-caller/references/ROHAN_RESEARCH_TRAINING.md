# Rohan's Training Handbook — how to do user research (read + practice BEFORE your first visit)

> This teaches you the **skill of asking** so you come back with the truth about how teaching really
> works. Read it, practice the role-plays with Deepak once, then carry `FIELD_RESEARCH_GUIDE.md` as
> your field script. Take your time with this — a well-prepared visit is worth ten rushed ones.

---

## PART 1 · Why this is the most important job in the company

You are TrigunAI's **eyes and ears on the ground.** We are not trying to push a product — we are
trying to *understand the real problems* teachers and institutes face, so we can build the right
solution (Acharya) for them. **Everything we build depends on the quality of what you bring back.**

- If you bring back the **truth** → we build what teachers actually need → the company wins.
- If you bring back "sab theek hai" or "unhe app chahiye" (vague) → we build the wrong thing → we lose.

So your job on a visit is **not** "did I sell?" It's **"did I truly understand this teacher's world
and their biggest pain — with real detail and real numbers?"** A visit where you learned a deep,
specific problem is a **success**, even if they never buy. Deepak needs your findings more than a sale.

---

## PART 2 · The mindset — how to think before you walk in

Put on the researcher's posture, not the salesman's:
- **You're a student, not a seller.** Walk in to *learn*: "Sir, main aapse seekhne aaya hoon aapke
  padhane ke baare mein — kuch bech nahi raha." This one sentence changes everything — people open up
  to a curious learner, they close up to a seller.
- **They are the expert, not you.** They've run their coaching for years. Be genuinely curious about
  how they do it. Respect it.
- **Don't defend Acharya. Don't even mention it early.** You're testing whether our idea fits *their*
  reality — not convincing them it does. Keep an open mind that maybe it doesn't (that's useful too).
- **Be comfortable with silence.** After they answer, wait 2–3 seconds. They'll often add the most
  honest, important thing in that gap. Don't rush to fill it.
- **Leave your ego at the door.** If they say something wrong about AI, don't correct them — *note it*.
  Their wrong belief IS the data (it's an objection we must handle).

---

## PART 3 · The 5 rules of good asking (learn these by heart)

1. **Talk about THEIR life, not our idea.** Never bring up Acharya until the very end. The whole
   conversation is about *them* — their students, their day, their headaches.
2. **Ask about the PAST, not the future.** "Kal kitne doubts aaye aur aapne kaise handle kiye?" gives
   truth. "Kya aapko AI tutor chahiye?" gives a polite lie — people say yes to be nice. Facts of last
   week beat opinions about tomorrow, every time.
3. **Listen 80%, talk 20%.** Your job is to make *them* talk. Every minute you're talking, you're not
   learning. Ask a short question, then be quiet.
4. **Chase the "why" and the number.** Every pain, dig 3 layers: "Aur?" → "Matlab?" → "Isme roz kitna
   time/paisa jaata hai?" A pain with a number attached is gold. A vague complaint is useless.
5. **Capture their EXACT words.** Write "raat ko 10 baje bachhe doubt bhejte हैं, main so jaata hoon,
   subah tak bhool jaate हैं" — not "doubt problem". Their words are the data.

---

## PART 4 · How to run the conversation (the flow)

Follow the sections in `FIELD_RESEARCH_GUIDE.md`, in this order:
1. **Open + rapport (2 min):** who you are, why you're here (to *learn*, not sell), ask permission for
   "10 minutes about how you teach."
2. **Their world:** subjects, students, solo-or-institute, fees, years. (Easy, warms them up.)
3. **Their day:** walk them through *yesterday* — where do the hours go besides teaching?
4. **The pain:** what's the most draining/time-eating part? Rank it. **Put a number on it.**
5. **What they already do/pay for:** their current workaround + what they spend.
6. **The dream + objections:** the one job they'd outsource; what worries them about AI.
7. **Close:** open catch-all ("koi aur badi problem?") + referrals. *Only if* a real pain clearly fits
   Acharya → offer the free pilot. Otherwise thank them warmly and leave. **Both are a win.**

---

## PART 5 · The skill of asking — the techniques

- **Open questions, not yes/no.** "Doubt kaise handle karte हैं?" (opens them up) beats "Doubt mein
  problem hoti hai?" (a dead-end yes/no that also *leads* them).
- **Probe deeper with 4 magic follow-ups:** *"Aur?"* · *"Matlab?"* · *"Phir kya hua?"* · *"Kyun?"*
  Most gold comes on the 2nd or 3rd probe, not the first answer.
- **Use silence.** Ask, then shut up. Count to 3 in your head. Let them fill it.
- **Quantify everything.** "Kitna time?" "Kitne students?" "Kitni baar?" "Kitna kharcha?" Numbers turn
  a story into data Deepak can act on.
- **Read the signals:**
  - They lean in, get animated, tell a long story → **real pain. Dig here.**
  - Short polite answers, "haan theek hai" → not a real pain. Move on.
  - They complain about something *unprompted* → that's their true #1. Follow it.
- **Never argue, never pitch mid-research.** If they say "AI galat padhata hai," don't defend — say
  "achha, aur kya lagta hai?" and note it. You're collecting beliefs, not correcting them.

## PART 6 · Learn by contrast — bad question vs good question

| ❌ Bad question | Why it's bad | ✅ Good question |
|---|---|---|
| "Kya aapko AI tutor chahiye?" | hypothetical + polite-yes | "Class ke baad students doubt kaise poochte हैं? Kal kitne aaye?" |
| "Doubt solve karna mushkil hai na?" | leading — begs a "haan" | "Pichhle hafte doubts kaise handle kiye? Kitne reh gaye?" |
| "Kya aap app use karenge?" | future guess | "Pehle koi app/tool liya tha? Kyun chhoda?" |
| "Aapko kaunse features chahiye?" | asks them to design our product | "Ek assistant ek kaam kar de roz — wo kaun sa hoga?" |
| "Ye problem badi hai na?" | seeking agreement, not truth | "Isme roz kitna time jaata hai? Isse kya nuksaan hota hai?" |
| "Hamara Acharya ye kar sakta hai…" | pitching = truth stops | (say nothing about Acharya — keep asking about them) |
| "Sab theek chal raha hai?" | invites "haan sab theek" | "Padhane ke alawa sabse zyada time kis cheez mein jaata hai?" |

**The pattern:** bad questions are about the *future*, our *product*, or seek a *yes*. Good questions
are about their *past*, their *behavior*, and stay *neutral*.

---

## PART 7 · Signal vs noise (what to trust)

- 🟢 **Signal (trust it):** a specific story + a number ("30 doubts roz, aadhe reh jaate हैं"); an
  unprompted complaint; a student who *left*; something they already *pay* for; an intro to another teacher.
- 🔴 **Noise (don't trust it):** compliments ("achha idea hai"), "haan zaroor lenge" without action,
  vague agreement, you talking. **A compliment is not data. A commitment (their time, a referral,
  a yes-to-pilot) is.**

---

## PART 8 · Practice — role-play these with Deepak before your first real visit

Do each once out loud. Deepak plays the teacher.
1. **The busy owner** ("2 minute hai bhai") → use THE ONE question (guide, top). Get one deep pain + a number.
2. **The skeptical owner** ("AI se padhai nahi hoti") → don't argue; dig *why* he thinks that; note the objection.
3. **The friendly owner** (talks a lot, off-topic) → gently steer back with "achha, aur class ke baad…?"; get the numbers.

**Pre-visit checklist:** ☐ I know their subject/area ☐ I'll open as a learner, not seller ☐ I will NOT
mention Acharya until the end ☐ I have the capture template ready.

**Post-visit self-check (be honest):** ☐ Did I get their #1 pain in *their words*? ☐ Did I get a
*number* on it? ☐ Did I find what they already do/pay for? ☐ Did I avoid pitching too early? ☐ Did I
log it same-day? If any answer is "no", that's what to fix next visit.
